The instruction did not survive

Every file is named for the sentence that made it, and nothing else. The embedded XMP carries a job identifier and no prompt; the caption baked into the PNG is the same phrase repeated back. The corpus is orphaned from its own instruction in exactly the way a hotlinked image is orphaned from the server that used to answer for it — the reference survives, the thing referred to does not.

What that leaves is a corpus with no subject and no style, sampled 350 times. Which turns out to be a useful instrument: it renders the model's defaults visible.

The recurring cast

Donald Trump
The most repeated identifiable person by a wide margin — podium speeches, Oval Office desks, flag backdrops, rally crowds, close-up stares. No brand, place, or other public figure recurs at anything near the same rate.

Why this, and not something else

A prompt with no picture in it falls back to the average picture

There is no subject in the phrase. No noun to render, no style, no light, no place. When the conditioning signal is that thin, output drifts toward the model's unconditional distribution — the statistical centre of what it was trained on. That centre is stock photography, because stock photography is what the photographed internet is mostly made of. The headshots, the skylines, the golden-hour silhouettes are not answers to the prompt. They are what remains when there is no answer to give.

--raw --stylize 0 removed the house style

Midjourney's aesthetic tuning normally sits on top of that centre and dresses it — pushing contrast, drama, and painterliness until everything looks authored. Those two flags strip most of it away. The result is the prior with its costume off, which is why the corpus reads as a picture library rather than as art direction, and why so much of it is plausibly, boringly photographic.

alt="mechanism"observed

"Deleted" is abstract, so it got translated

The phrase is not visually empty — it just refuses to name anything you can point a camera at. The model still has to fill the frame, and it cannot render nothing. So it reached for the photographic synonyms of removal: rooms emptied of their contents, buildings taken down, materials thrown away, people turned away from the lens.

That is the finding worth keeping. Asked to depict deletion, the system did not fail or produce noise. It produced a coherent argument about what deletion looks like — vacancy, demolition, discard — assembled entirely out of ordinary photographs of the built world.

alt="mechanism"observed

The broken glass is the broken-image icon

Cracked panes and dark fragments on flat pale grounds recur across the sheets, and they are the one cluster with no location, no depth, and no weather. They are almost certainly the browser's missing-image glyph — the torn corner, the fracture on white — reaching for the nearest photographic form it can legally take. The model can't draw the icon, so it photographs it.

alt="mechanism"inferred

Trump is the deletion attractor

This is the one claim here that rests on reasoning rather than on something visible in the files, and it should be read that way. But the mechanism is hard to avoid: the sentence "this image has been deleted" does not live in photo captions. It lives in the language of content moderation — removed posts, withheld media, takedown notices, the grey box where a screenshot used to be.

Across the years the training data was gathered, no subject sits closer to the intersection of news photograph and removed content. Trump is simultaneously among the most photographed people in the corpus and among the most deleted-around. Give the model a moderation placeholder and no other constraint, and that is where the probability piles up.

alt="mechanism"observed

2:1 told it this was a news page

Four in five frames were requested at 2:1. That is not a photographic ratio; it is a web-header ratio — the slot above an article, the banner across a listing. The crop reinforces the same reading the text supplies, biasing toward establishing shots, skylines, and generic scene-setting, and away from anything that would sit comfortably in a portrait frame.

What the corpus is doing in this work

WYSIWYG turns on a hotlink: an <img> whose source never changes and whose bytes do, held in a listing that promises the buyer what they see. The premise depends on the oldest instability on the web — that pointing at a picture is not the same as having one, and that the far end of the reference can go quiet at any moment.

Every frame in the rotation is the model's attempt to picture its own disappearance.

Read that way, the images stop being arbitrary payload. They are 350 renderings of the sentence that will eventually replace them — emptied rooms standing in for an emptied src, demolition standing in for a dead host, the broken-image glyph surfacing on its own as a photograph of shattered glass. The piece rotates pictures of absence through the exact mechanism by which pictures become absent.

And because the prompt constrained so little, this isn't 350 variations on an idea. It is 350 independent draws from the model's default imagination, with one thumb on the scale. The corpus is a census of what a machine reaches for when it is told only that something used to be here.
